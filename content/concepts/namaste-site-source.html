The mission

To procure equines seized by law enforcement, rehabilitate them mentally and physically, and re-home them in a safe, forever environment.

Thousands of equines every year are mentally and physically neglected. When a Wisconsin department steps in, the horse still needs somewhere to go that same day — a barn, a vet, a farrier, and someone with the time to sit with an animal that has learned not to trust people.

That is what this farm is for.

Read our story

How it works

Three stages, and none of them are quick.

A seizure is the beginning of the paperwork and the beginning of the horse's real problems. Recovery is measured in seasons, not weeks.

Law enforcement calls

A Wisconsin department seizes an equine and needs somewhere for it to go. We take the intake, open a file, and start a record that follows the horse for the rest of its life.

Weight, feet, and trust

Shelter, food, vet and farrier care, and unconditional love and attention. Refeeding is done slowly and on purpose. Hooves that have gone years without a trim come back over many cycles. The mental side usually takes longest.

The home that keeps them

Adopters are visited before approval, and we stay in touch for the lifetime of the horse. Adopted equines cannot be sold on, leased, or loaned without our approval. That is the whole point of the file.

The part nobody expects

The rescue was created in 2014 to help law enforcement with seized equines. What we learned was that 99% of our purpose was to educate horse owners — turning a seizure into a more positive experience of education and knowledge, so that the next horse never has to be seized at all.

So we built the lesson into this website: the six places you put your hands on a horse, and why you must never simply feed a starved one.

About

Created for law enforcement. Kept going by education.

Namaste Equine Rescue was created in 2014 to originally help law enforcement that had seized equines. What we found in the years since changed what this place is for.

The mission, in full

To procure equines seized by law enforcement, primarily serving the state of Wisconsin, to rehabilitate them mentally and physically and re-home them in a safe, forever environment.

We provide shelter, food, vet or farrier care, and unconditional love and attention. Those four things sound simple written down. In practice they are the entire budget, the entire calendar, and most of the reason volunteers are needed.

We also commit to educating law enforcement and owners on proper management, to further protect equines in this state.

What we learned

99% of our purpose was to educate horse owners.

We started out expecting to be a destination — the barn at the end of a bad situation. What became obvious was that most of these cases were not malice. They were owners in over their heads, and nobody had ever shown them what a healthy horse looks like.

So the work turned into turning a seizure into a more positive experience of education and knowledge. The horse still gets rehabilitated. But the owner learns something, the department learns something, and sometimes the next horse never gets seized at all.

Learn

Ninety-nine percent of this job is teaching people to read a horse.

That is not a slogan, it is what this rescue found out. Most neglect cases are not malice — they are an owner who never learned what a healthy horse feels like under the hand. So here is the thing we actually want you to take away from this website.

The Henneke scale

Score a horse in six places.

Body condition is not a guess and it is not a look. It is six places you put your hands, each scored one to nine, then averaged. The six places are marked here on Captain, a horse who came through this farm. What each of the nine scores looks like is further down the page.

There are two sets of numbers here, and they are not the same thing. 1–6 on Captain are the six places you put your hands. 1–9 below are the nine scores — how much cover you find when you get there.

What your hand is pressing through

A score is a feel, not a look.

Relative depths, drawn to show the idea rather than to measure it. Fat is the only layer that changes: the bone stays exactly where it was, which is why a hand on the same six places tells you the truth about a horse when your eye will not.

    Now do it yourself

    Score a horse.

    Put a hand on all six places and give each one a number from one to nine. Do not average as you go and do not let one bad patch decide it — score each place on its own, then let the six of them settle it.

    What we ask of a foster home

    Never below a 4.

    Every horse placed with a Namaste foster home has to be kept at a minimum of 4 on this scale, monitored daily and written down. That number is on our foster agreement, and now you know exactly what it means.

    Foster requirements

    The scale was developed by Don Henneke at Texas A&M University and first published in 1983. It is scored by sight and by hand — a winter coat hides a great deal, which is why January cases are so often missed. Score all six areas separately and average them; one thin patch is not a thin horse.

    This page is here to teach, not to diagnose. If a horse you own scores 3 or below, that is a veterinary call, today — not a feeding decision you make on your own.

    The part everybody gets wrong

    You cannot just feed a starved horse.

    This is the single most useful thing on this website. The instinct — put food in front of it — is the instinct that kills. Reintroducing feed too fast triggers refeeding syndrome: insulin drives minerals and electrolytes out of the bloodstream and the horse can go down with edema, diarrhea, neurological signs or heart failure. It does not come from one meal. It builds over the first days to a week, right when everybody thinks the rescue is going well.

    Days 1–3

    One pound of alfalfa, every four hours

    Six small feeds a day, roughly half of what the horse actually needs. No grain at all. Good leafy alfalfa, because it produces a lower glucose and insulin response than grain or grass — which is precisely what you are trying to avoid.

    Days 4–5

    Two to three pounds, every four hours

    Still six feeds. Still no grain. About three quarters of requirement.

    Days 6–10

    Four to five pounds, every four hours

    Full requirement, and the feeds start consolidating toward three a day.

    Day 10 onward

    Slowly, for months

    Hay every eight to twelve hours, increasing gradually. Grain is not introduced until body condition has come up off the floor, and then in half-pound meals.

    Before you apply

    What we need to see.

    None of this is about wealth. It is about whether the horse in front of you will be safe, fed, and looked at every single day.

    • Prior experience owning equines.
    • At least 18 years old.
    • Living in Wisconsin or nearby. Out-of-state cases are evaluated individually.
    • Boarding at an approved facility, or keeping the animal on your own property.
    • A barn or a lean-to, safe and proper fencing — board or wire mesh is appropriate, no barbed wire.
    • Adequate water and feed supply.
    • Contact details for your veterinarian, farrier, and caregiver.
    • Willingness to let a Namaste representative visit before approval.

    The process

    Four steps, at the horse's pace.

    Print and submit the form

    Print out the adoption form and submit it by mail, or email it to namasteequinerescue@gmail.com. You do not have to name a horse.

    We talk, and we visit

    A Namaste representative visits the premises before any approval. We look at fencing, shelter, water, and where the horse would actually stand on a bad February night.

    You receive the file

    Approved adopters get a file on the animal: how it came to be on the farm, what veterinary care it received while it was here, and what the staff has personally noticed about it.

    We stay in touch, for good

    Periodic checks may be made by a Namaste representative for the lifetime of the horse. Adopted animals cannot be moved on, leased, or loaned without our approval.

    Questions we get

    Is there an adoption fee?

    Fees are handled case by case — ask us when you submit your application and we will be straight with you about what this particular horse has cost to bring back.

    I have never adopted a rescue horse. Am I out?

    No. We ask for prior equine ownership experience, not prior rescue experience. What matters is that you can read a horse and keep a routine.

    Why the lifetime check-ins?

    Because most of these animals were failed once already. The follow-up is not distrust of you; it is the promise we made to the horse.

    Can I adopt from out of state?

    Possibly. We serve Wisconsin primarily, and out-of-state applications are evaluated individually.
